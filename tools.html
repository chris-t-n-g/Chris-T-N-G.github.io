Mapping Digital Access and Demographic Interactions in LA

Part of the LA Data Justice Hub

This mapping tool visualizes how digital access—both internet and computers—interacts with demographic variables in LA County. It serves as an introduction to data justice issues, addressing historical and current concerns about equitable representation and digital infrastructure. The LA Data Justice Hub aims to build on this tool to explore and address data inequities and opportunities for justice.

Veriform [prototype] - Verifyable, Anonymous Petition Signing Program

Anonymous yet verifiable petition-signing platform inspired by UCLA graduate students who felt unable to openly share their opinions on protests due to fears of institutional or popular backlash. By leveraging a unique token and QR verification process, Veriform ensures that every signature is both legitimate and traceable—without revealing personal information. Its local file storage architecture prioritizes privacy, while the system's built-in identity checks guarantee each signer is a unique member of the UCLA community. This combination of anonymity and verifiability empowers users to safely and confidently participate in collective decisions and advocacy.

Own Thy Face [prototype] - Crowdsourced mapping tool to support and incentivize reappropriation of personal data

OTF is a GIS-powered web app that incentivizes LA residents to crowdsource CCTV camera locations and photo evidence. Users earn tokens by documenting and verifying surveillance infrastructure, and the platform automatically generates formal letters—addressed to the relevant providers, companies, or city officials—requesting acknowledgment and reappropriation of captured personal data. By aligning with GDPR and CCPA guidelines, this system empowers community members to better understand and advocate for their privacy rights.

Oct 20

When I got to 'Mercuh, I suffered the surprise of writing assessments that prioritized formulaic writing. #creativeDeath4Life

To be fair, my writing wasn't all that clear: my hubris reached for big ideas, and I had not paid too much attention in my high school English class. I must say, however, that I am still quite resentful about what seems like a slightly dogmatic fanaticism with “accessibility” and “clarity” in our writing.

Mill talks about a sort of cognitive sovereignty in the soliloquies where he complains about the tyrannies of the herd. Call me paranoid, but much of the same creative death that GPT is blamed for seems to have its precursor in the tyranny of accessible writing. I am not sure writing is most “generative” when it is tailored to an audience... at all.

Orthogonally, Bateson tells us that the term “information” is any difference that makes a difference (maybe it was MacKay or something—not sure). And, yeah, sure, I get that most academic writing, even when it is accessible, has an audience that is highly specialized, and finds diversity by way of being disciplined by the discipline—if you catch my drift (vroom vroom). Nevertheless, I wouldn't be surprised if the fanaticism with accessibility hampers diversity by formalizing such conventions even across disciplinary writing conventions.

Warner's text reminded me of that thought. Prioritizing ideas, perhaps even at the expense of clarity—Thank God, someone said it! Yet, taking a step back to, like Professor X, summon our metacognitive powers, Warner also reminds me of a troubling pattern which is particularly prevalent in the humanities. We claim to be so critical and yet fall into monolithic conclusions in our “critical thinking.” “AI bad,” “AI stops our thinking for sure,” “bla bla bla,” and not only with AI. The root of “criticism,” or the Proto-Indo-European krei (womp, womp!), is related to the concept of separating, discerning, splitting, and difference. Going back to Bateson, it has to do with the genration of information. If we are so lucid in our moralized conclusions, be they in writing, knowledge production, or politics, then where the hellie is the “difference” that makes a difference?
